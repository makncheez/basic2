“The fleeting quality of fame is a well-known concept to any scholar of history. Recognition and renown can fade, just as an old-format photographic image will ‘wash-out’ through overexposure. A few names stand the test of time, but most are lost, except to those who knew the individual personally or who are devoted students of the individual’s particular field.”
— Mehmed Ali

Aram Kazanjian (ca. 1878–1966)
held a prominent place in the history of photography. He shaped the world of commercial portraiture in Boston and New York. Like many of the Armenian photographers who rose to prominence during the early part of the 20th century, Kazanjian’s photographic oeuvre and renown have faded into obscurity and lost in the mists of time. To celebrate his life and legacy, this retrospective highlights Kazanjian’s prolific photographic output during a dynamic era in the medium’s history.

The History of Armenian Photography in Boston

Kazanjian’s career emerged and evolved during the early 20th century in Boston. Beyond the camera’s ability to accurately record reality and to produce candid portraiture, there was an aesthetic movement referred to as Pictorialism. This movement aimed to establish photography as fine art. Pictorialist photographers manipulated an image “to express their artistic and abstract ideas” and their “subjective points of view.” Influenced by Impressionism, the 19th century art movement that began in France, photographers practicing Pictorialism employed soft-focus techniques and manipulated light to create compositions that were aesthetically pleasing and “painterly in style.” The ultimate goal was to elevate photography to the rank of fine art. Along with the practice of producing straightforward portraiture, Kazanjian was influenced by this aesthetic movement that created photographic images rather than simply recording them. As such, his oeuvre reflected a blend of varied photographic styles that evolved around the turn of the 20th century.

Boston became a hub for Pictorialism which promoted photography as fine art. Amongst the influx of Armenian immigrants in this rapidly growing diasporan community, those who were entrepreneurial, artistically creative, and trained to practice photography established successful commercial portrait studios. Armenians were widely known to have mastered photography throughout the Ottoman Empire; this reputation not only made assimilation easier into American society, but it also facilitated their efforts to open studios in the major diaspora hubs of Boston and New York.

Along with Kazanjian, two prominent Armenian studios that represented disparate styles in photographic practice around the turn of the 20th century were the Diran Studio run by Suren and Arsen Diran, and the Garo Studio established by John Higue Garo.

Diran Studio

Arsen Diran arrived as an Armenian émigré from Kharpert, Turkey to the Boston area around 1890. He opened a commercial studio sometime around the early 1890s. Whether he had an artistic background or photographic training in Turkey remains unknown. His focus was the production of straightforward, traditional portraits which were often portrayed by subjects in stiff and constricted poses, painted backdrops, and distinctive props to complement or enhance the main subject. Following standard printing techniques, these images were affixed onto decorative mounting cards. Around the early 1900s, Suren Diran (1881–1932) joined his older brother, Arsen. Together, they ran the studio until the late 1920s when Arsen moved to Fresno, California. The studio remained open until Suren’s passing in 1932. Much to his dismay during the last years of his life, the studio was adversely impacted by a massive drop in clients during the Great Depression.

As a traditional commercial studio, the Diran studio used the camera as a recording tool that prioritized meticulously composed, sharp, and standardized portraits. Their work stands in contrast to Pictorialism which infused images with a creative vision that evoked an emotional mood. Diran’s portraiture did not rely on variations in lighting, blurred imagery, painterly techniques, nor experimentation with complex printing methods.

Garo Studio

Born in Kharpert, Turkey, John Higue Garo (1870–1939) landed in Worcester, Massachusetts at the age of 14. With his artistic proclivities and penchant for painting, he immersed himself in the study of the arts and the photographic medium. As the proprietor of an upscale portrait studio in Boston, frequented by members of Boston’s elite society, Garo emerged as one of the most celebrated and gifted pictorialist photographers in New England. The renowned Armenian photographer and Garo’s mentee, Yousuf Karsh, regarded him as “one of the greatest photographers in the history of the art.” During the early 20th century, Garo was the recipient of many national and international honors and awards for his artistic photographs. Many of his portraits were published in prestigious American publications like The Spur which catered to affluent high society.

A study of Garo’s photographs illustrates his reliance on the aesthetic quality set forth by Impressionism and Art Nouveau (1890–1914) which emphasized soft flowing imagery and natural light. This artistic style influenced him to create photographs of visual beauty that emphasized his subject’s personality and their emotional state. Using soft flowing curves and soft-focus techniques to create blur, Garo was motivated to create a work of art rather than to record it. In his estimation, photography was more than a technical practice to record an image, but an artistic pursuit to make photographs look like paintings or etchings. Described as “a renaissance man with many colors on his palette,” Garo regarded photography as a true form of art which exceeded all other artistic mediums.

“The one outstanding characteristic of photography—the quality that distinguishes it from drawing, painting, etching, or any other means of pictorial representation—is the ability of the lens and sensitive film to record infinitely delicate and subtle gradation of tones. This is where photography is supreme and it is because of this quality that photography claims and holds a special place among the fine arts.”

— John Higue Garo

By the 1930s, Garo did not make the necessary adjustments in his practice to meet the rapid changes occurring in the photographic medium. His acclaimed and innovative technical achievements, like the gum arabic technique which created a painterly effect, became obsolete. Under the influence of the Art Deco movement (1920s–1940s), commercial photography shifted towards streamlined, simpler forms and high contrast in lighting. As such, “the days of the photo’s soft edge and ethereal mood had yielded to the sharp realism practiced by the modernists in the field.”

“I have enjoyed every minute of my life. Photography is my vocation and I love it because I find in it the medium to interpret the art of painting, the art of music, the art of classical literature. But greatest of all, it is my canvas for the fine art of living. Art is everywhere if one can see, hear or feel it. But most of all, it is in life.”

— John Higue Garo
“Untitled,” 1911. Garo Studio, Boston. Yearbook of the Photographers’ Association of New England, Bridgeport Convention.

Aram Kazanjian

As a sixteen-year-old Armenian émigré from Erzincan, Turkey, Aram Kazanjian (ca. 1878–January 1966) arrived in the Boston area around 1895. With a population of 19,026 around 1880, Armenians from the sanjak of Erzincan within the Erzurum vilayet were known for their rich culture of monasteries, schools, and active trade networks. Leaving behind the vibrant community of Erzincan, Kazanjian was sent to Paris to study painting and the arts. His move to Boston was prompted by the tragic news that his parents were killed during the Hamidian Armenian massacres (1894-1896). While in Paris one night, Kazanjian dreamt that his family’s house in Erzincan had been burned down to the ground. In the morning upon awakening, he learned the terrifying truth of the Turkish massacres. His father and mother had indeed been killed. A return home was impossible in the face of this traumatic event. Kazanjian fled to America with his cousin Varaztad Kazanjian who became a renowned oral surgeon and the founder of the modern practice of plastic surgery.

Tremont Street, Boston, Massachusetts, 1904. Courtesy of the Malikian Collection.

Around the turn of the 20th century, Boston underwent a period of significant demographic shifts and modernization. This transformation was characterized by remarkable developments and rapid expansion, such as the opening of the Tremont Street Subway in 1897 which was the first underground transit system in the United States. Within this transformative period of revitalization in Boston, Kazanjian—who came to America as a traumatized indigent immigrant—found success both personally and professionally.

On January 17, 1907, Kazanjian married Leah Ethel Davis, daughter of the actor Albert Davis. It was Kazanjian’s artistic background and his playful marketing strategy that brought his future wife to the studio.

“It was through his profession that Daddy met my mother. My aunt had heard about this photographer, whom she described as dark, handsome, mysterious, twirling fierce black moustachios, who painted birds, bees and butterflies on a lady’s shoulder or cheek when he photographed her! Giggling with the wickedness of it all, my mother and aunt went over to have their pictures taken, and sure enough, Father painted a butterfly on Mother’s shoulder.”

“When mother wandered into the studio with my aunt, he must have really been inspired. Taking note of her porcelain beauty—the fragile of her transparent skin, the wide, blue eyes, the shimmering gold hair—he painted the most delicate butterfly of his career and began his courtship on the spot by asking permission to call on her.”

— Arlene Kazanjian Francis

Kazanjian’s romantic pursuit to impress Leah Davis led to fifty-nine years of marriage and a daughter, Arlene Kazanjian Francis (October 20, 1907–May 31, 2001), who became a renowned radio, television, film, and stage actress. Francis described her father as a stern, old-world, overly protective, and “archaic” paternal figure. Yet, this “strait-laced,” conventional Armenian father was undeniably an “adorable” man committed to his family, and passionate about his artistic endeavors.

Boston: The Early Years,
1900–1913

Following an apprenticeship with a photographer in the Boston area, Kazanjian opened his first commercial photographic studios on 160 Tremont Street and on 74 Boylston Street which he named “Aram.” His earlier portraits were thematically conventional like those produced by many other studios in Boston, such as the Diran Studio run by his Armenian compatriots, Arsen and Suren Diran.

It is unknown who mentored Kazanjian. However, given elements of Pictorialism and Art Nouveau in Kazanjian’s 1915-1920s portraits of prominent figures, it is conceivable that Garo not only served as an important influence, but he may have mentored him as well. Kazanjian’s artistic background might have brought him into Garo’s “salon” which was a gathering place for the cultural and artistic community in Boston. In Garo’s estimation, Armenian photographers such as Karsh and Kazanjian would have been regarded as “gifted artists and master technicians”

In 1905, Kazanjian’s photograph, “Modern Mother of God and Child Jesus,” was awarded the C.P. Goerz Silver Cup; an award that Garo also received in 1904 and 1906. Having won the Silver Cup three times, Garo was granted permanent possession of the silver trophy. As further evidence of parallels in Garo and Kazanjian’s work, both men submitted artistically composed painterly types of portraiture to luxury-focused publications, such as The Spur, which catered to older wealthy families of New York City and the Boston Brahmins.

The Harvard-Boston Aero Meet of 1910

During Kazanjian’s years in Boston, he stepped out of the studio to visually document the inaugural Harvard-Boston Aero Meet which took place from September 3-13, 1910. These documentary-style photographs captured a sequence of images that chronicled “the greatest meet of its kind in America which drew over 60,000 paid spectators.” Undoubtedly, these photographs, signed as “Aram,” added an important dimension to his portfolio. It gave him name recognition in the field of commercial and documentary photography.

Kazanjian produced and commercially distributed postcards that documented the events from the aviation meet of 1910. His photographs taken over the course of ten days not only supplemented his income and produced collectible postcards that were highly valued around the turn of the 20th century, but they also served as memorabilia of a captivating event in the history of American aviation.

The months of preparations for this momentous occasion heralded the dawn of modernity with its enhanced forms of advertising and promotion, coordination of vendors, the advanced construction of grandstands capable of seating 150,000 people, and parking areas which accommodated up to 10,000 automobiles.

Kazanjian’s photographs of The Harvard-Boston Aero Meet of 1910 were significant as they recorded the first international air meet of its kind held in the United States which drew the world’s top airmen of the day. On August 20, 1910, the New York Tribune reported:

“No aviation meet held in this country, and probably none yet held in the world, has had such a representative list of foremost aviators as is assured the Harvard-Boston Aero Meet according to the list of entrants to date, announced to-night. The entry list is international and includes seventeen individual aviators and eleven types of air navigating machines. The latter embrace the principal types – the monoplane, biplane, and triplane. It will be the first time the latter type has been exhibited in this country.”

— New York Tribune
Cromwell Dixon, the 18-year-old pilot who flew his own custom-built man-carrying dirigible, 1910. Aram Studio, Boston. Courtesy of the Malikian Collection.

To protect his photographs from unauthorized use and distribution, Kazanjian copyrighted them. Copyright mandates with photographs during the early 20th century were undergoing major revisions. In 1906, John Garo volunteered on a committee that worked with Congress to update the copyright laws affecting photography. At the time, the government mandated that the copyright symbol appear in the corner of a photograph. Garo took issue with this mandate since it “ruined the potential artistry of the image.” With Garo and his committee members’ efforts, the Copyright Act of 1909 was passed which ended this mandate, thereby allowing the copyright symbol to appear on the margin or the back of a photograph. Following the passage of this mandate, the copyright symbol on Kazanjian’s commercial photographs no longer appeared on the image but in the margin or on the back of his photographs.

New York: The Later Years, 1914–1950s

In 1914, Kazanjian decided to relocate to New York City as it offered greater opportunities for success. Undoubtedly, it proved to be a good move professionally.

Between 1915 to 1920 and thereafter, New York City went through a fast-paced era of modern transformation. Iconic skyscrapers like the Woolworth Building reshaped the skyline, the subway system expanded into outer boroughs, and diverse immigrant communities fueled a thriving cultural scene on Broadway. Culturally, this era was a renaissance period for New York City as the arts scene flourished. The Roaring Twenties set in motion several years of glamour and wealth as the city grew into a strong financial and industrial center. This growth set the stage for its role as a global metropolis and a major city in the 20th century.

Fifth Avenue at 42nd Street, New York City, 1912. Courtesy of the Malikian Collection.

Within this dynamic and progressive era in New York City, Kazanjian found enormous success. Along with his partner, Frances Kaiden, they established one of the premier photographic studios in the city. Kazanjian and Kaiden moved the studio four times, but it remained primarily in the Upper East Side along 5th Avenue. Within this world-famous corridor of New York City’s elite wealth, grand architecture and cultural landmarks, the studio attracted many affluent patrons with social standing in the arts, politics and the business world. The studio not only attracted affluent members of New York society but also budding photographers in search of training and experience with the medium. Around 1915, the renowned documentary photographer and pioneer in socially committed photography, Dorothea Lange, worked as an assistant at the Kazanjian studio while taking photography classes at Columbia University.

During the early years in New York, Kazanjian specialized in children’s photographs. He became very successful and garnered the reputation of being one of the best-known men in the field. Some of his earlier works used elements of Pictorialism and Art Nouveau to produce photographs with a painterly effect. To achieve this result, these romanticized and idealized compositions emphasized the use of soft-focus techniques and the manipulation of light.

With the passage of time and transformations in the photographic medium, Kazanjian embraced some of the newer elements of image making from the Art Deco movement. This new trend diverged from the artistic, painterly style emphasized by Pictorialism. Art Deco trends in photography came about in the late 1920s and 1930s with the use of bold geometric shapes, sharp lines, dramatic shadows, and a sleek modern style to mirror the architecture and fashion of the machine age. Some of these elements were incorporated into Kazanjian’s photographs.

The Kaiden-Kazanjian Studio vs. The Federal Trade Commission

Amid Kaiden-Kazanjian Studio’s success, on August 14, 1942, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) filed a complaint against them, along with its principals, for deceptively presenting commercial portrait work as legitimate news and press photography. Operating under the name Keystone News and Photo Service, the Kaiden-Kazanjian company was accused of misrepresenting its business practices.

Kaiden-Kazanjian Studio Inc., New York. The studio was presented as a “Division of Keystone News and Photo Service” before June 16, 1943.

As per the alleged violation of Section 5 of the Act of Congress established in 1914, The New York Times reported on August 20, 1942, that the Federal Trade Commission charged the studio, a corporation, Frances Kaiden, as president and secretary of Kaiden-Kazanjian Studios, Inc., and Aram Kazanjian, as vice president and treasurer of Kaiden-Kazanjian Studios, Inc. for trading individually as Keystone News & Photo Service. Although the studio may have sold a very small percentage of photographs to various media outlets, the FTC complaint ultimately found that the studio and principals were falsely claiming to operate an independent news and photo agency, and that they used deceptive representations, such as pretending to manage official press publicity to market commercial photography services. Furthermore, the studio was cited for some other advertising practices that were misleading and false. On June 16, 1943, the studio was required to cease-and-desist from all such practices. It was the FTC’s decision and conclusion that:

“The aforesaid acts and practices of the respondents as herein found are all to the prejudice and injury of the public and constitutes unfair and deceptive acts and practices in commerce within the intent and meaning of the Federal Trade Commission Act.”

— Federal Trade Commission

To what extent Kazanjian was directly involved in corporate malfeasance for false advertising remains unclear. From some of the images attributed to “Kaiden-Keystone Photos,” it appears that Frances Kaiden may have been the party primarily responsible for promoting this form of advertisement. It is questionable whether Kazanjian passively and naively supported Kaiden’s dubious marketing strategies as well as his deceptive advertising malpractices. By virtue of his association with the Kaiden-Kazanjian Studio, he was implicated by the FTC. The degree to which the complaints and ruling were a dark stain on Kazanjian’s business reputation remains unknown.

Kazanjian’s Contributions to the Elite American Publications

During the early 20th century, high-society magazines, such as Vogue, The Spur, Town and Country, transformed and elevated photography into fine art. Many of the photographs represented in these magazines championed Pictorialism and Art Nouveau. The photographer’s artistically creative eye produced compelling compositions that transcended mere technical skill. While some photographs recorded a moment in time, it was the Pictorialist’s vision and intent that made many of these portraits a work of art. Delicate backlighting, soft lenses, and dreamy or gently blurred backdrops were used to create romanticized, impressionistic portraits of wealthy aristocratic families.

Kazanjian’s involvement with luxury-focused publications gave him a firm foothold within a niche audience of affluent high society residing in New York City and its environs. These publications featured society news and events, women’s fashion, culture, traditions of high society, and social etiquette. Along with Kazanjian’s visually striking artistic portraits, he chronicled weddings, debutante balls, and leisure activities of the upper class.

A study of Kazanjian’s contributions to Vogue, The Spur, Town and Country affirm his status as one of the leading photographers to elevate photography to the level of fine art.

The Twilight Years

From Kazanjian’s teenage years, which were devoted to studying fine arts in Paris, through adulthood, his devotion to painting became an important creative outlet. His artistic proclivities and passion for painting heavily influenced his illustrious photography career. He brought to photography his imaginative and aesthetic vision. As such, his photographic compositions often looked less like a simple record of a moment in time but more like a thoughtful piece of fine art.

Today, many of Kazanjian’s paintings are scattered and their whereabouts are unknown. As shown by three samples of his work in this exhibit, he painted a spectrum of subjects, ranging from quiet rural landscapes to portraits. His cumulative and vigorous artistic drive to paint as a personal hobby reduced the family’s living space, to the extent that the walls were all covered with his paintings. “My recollections of childhood are all tied up with trying to find a light switch, so completely covered with his paintings were our walls,” noted Arlene Kazanjian Francis. To maximize the family’s living space, some of his lesser paintings were auctioned sometime in the 1950s at Plaza Art Galleries established by the O’Reilly brothers on the Upper East Side of New York City.

Kazanjian, Aram. Snowy winter landscape with sunset, ca. 1920. Oil on canvas. Courtesy of the Malikian Collection.

While Kazanjian enjoyed painting in retirement, too much free time left him unfulfilled, even with hobbies he once treasured. His retirement was seemingly premature; he was still physically and mentally fit to work. Tormented by his lack of productivity amid a fierce work ethic, Kazanjian grappled with deep guilt. His relentless work ethic stemmed from a drive forged by early losses, scarcity, and displacement from his homeland.

“He had never learned how to cope with leisure—not in large doses. You have to have a special talent for that, one which you don’t develop if you are reared with a ‘work ethic’ philosophy which dictates that it is sinful not to improve each shining hour”

— Arlene Kazanjian Francis

With his premature retirement, Kazanjian became severely withdrawn by the absence of work that once gave him purpose and meaning. Periods of dysphoria eventually worsened with the onset of dementia, marked by alarming forgetfulness, disorientation, and a rapid flight of ideas. His declining health forced the family to make the heartbreaking decision to place him in a nursing home, where he lived until he passed away in January 1966.

Legacy

Reflecting on Kazanjian’s tangible legacy and impact, his photographic oeuvre and renown may have faded with the passage of time and changes within the photographic medium. Yet, as with some of the earlier accomplished Armenian photographers, such as John Higue Garo, Kazanjian’s artistic background and passion for the visual arts shaped how he took pictures, turning them into dramatic and carefully balanced works of art.

“If we are to believe that history progresses forward, that humanity is built upon the foundation of our forerunners, then we need to recognize those who preceded us”
— Mehmed Ali
